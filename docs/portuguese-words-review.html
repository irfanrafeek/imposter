The Brazilian Portuguese word catalogue

All 550 words across seven categories, each with its three hints. This is the last part of the Portuguese that no native speaker has read. Everything else, 973 rows of interface and page copy, has been.

How the hints work, before you judge one

Every word carries three hints and the impostor is shown exactly one of them, picked fresh each round. That is why a hint looks vaguer than you might expect: it is the only thing the impostor gets, and a hint that names the thing hands them something safe to say.

  • Dica 1 and Dica 2 are the hard band. Abstract properties. A hard hint should fit dozens of words in its category.
  • Dica fácil is a concrete association, the situation the thing lives in rather than the thing. It should fit about five words. Never one.
  • Hints avoid adjectives that inflect. A gendered adjective beside a gendered noun narrows the answer sharply, so the catalogue leans on infinitive verbs, adjectives ending in -e, and concrete nouns. If a hint reads oddly stiff, that constraint is usually why, and the fix is a different word rather than a gendered one.
  • A hint must not name the thing, and the trap here is regional origin. Bahia on acarajé, or Minas on pão de queijo, identifies the dish outright.

What to mark

The catalogue was written for Brazil rather than for Portuguese in general, so the questions are about whether it lands at a Brazilian table.

  • The thing is Portuguese, not Brazilian. A dish, a place or a habit that belongs to Portugal and reads as imported here.
  • The thing is universal and the word is not. Comboio, autocarro, telemóvel, frigorífico, casa de banho. Not wrong Portuguese, but another country’s, and it reads as foreign at a Brazilian table.
  • The word does not travel inside Brazil. This is the one Spanish did not have. Mandioca, aipim and macaxeira are the same root in three regions. Everyone at the table sees the secret word and has to clue it, so a word half the room does not know stops the round rather than colouring it.
  • A hint gives the answer away, or fits only one word.
  • A hint is bookish. Gender-safe abstractions nobody says out loud, the -idade and -ura forms, are exactly what reads as machine-written.

Words tagged also in en appear in the English catalogue too. There are 43 of them and most are proper nouns that are the same everywhere. The catalogue was written rather than translated, so a long run of them in one category would be worth a look.
